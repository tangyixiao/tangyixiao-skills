# Academic writing and proposals

Maintain the discipline's register and the author's voice. Clear academic prose
can use technical terms, equations, first-person plural, and passive procedures.
An editing pass should improve access to the argument without making it casual.

## Match the claim to the evidence

Distinguish observed association, causal inference, theoretical proof, hypothesis,
and planned work. Keep the strength that the actual design supports. A proved
theorem can remain “proved”; observational evidence cannot become proof of a
causal mechanism. “Significant” needs the relevant statistical or contextual
support; it is not a decorative synonym for “large.”

Preserve exact values, uncertainty, units, sample sizes, baseline definitions,
evaluation conditions, figure/table references, and citation keys. Keep percentage
changes distinct from percentage-point differences. Do not replace a reported
average with a range or compare against a different baseline for rhetorical effect.

If support already exists in the supplied document, an evidence pointer may make
the claim easier to check. Do not invent a table, p-value, citation, or numerical
result. Flag an unsupported claim or narrow its language with a clear change note
when that changes the argument. Do not quietly delete a reference or adverse result.

## Organize around the contribution

State the concrete question, finding, mechanism, or capability. Remove ritual
modesty and generic novelty claims when they add no information. Preserve
“preliminary,” “suggests,” or “may” when they accurately describe the evidence.
Make contribution lists name distinct supported contributions.

Keep limitations near the claims they qualify when omission would mislead.
Methods, discussion, and limitations sections can hold repeated detail, but moving
a limitation must not make the abstract or conclusion falsely general. Describe
unfavorable findings and tradeoffs with their conditions and metrics.

For rebuttals, answer the reviewer's actual concern with supplied evidence.
Separate a demonstrated error, an open verification question, and an optional
experiment. Do not invent new experiments, completed fixes, or reviewer agreement.

## Proposals

Preserve ambition while distinguishing objectives, hypotheses, planned methods,
preliminary evidence, and expected outcomes. Match commitments to the resources,
track record, collaborators, and contingencies the author actually supplies.
Do not turn an aim into a completed result or promise success as a fact.

Help the opening explain the problem, gap, proposed approach, aims, and expected
value. Clarify dependencies among aims and any supplied fallback. Avoid inventing
feasibility evidence, partners, letters, funded history, or outreach programs.

Use the user's current call or venue instructions for binding structure. When
asked to check page limits, formatting, review criteria, or submission rules,
verify the current official requirements; this skill does not encode them.

## Illustrative examples

**Input:** “In our observational sample of 84 adults, X may potentially be associated
with Y (r = 0.31; 95% CI 0.10–0.49) [7].”

**Revision:** “In our observational sample of 84 adults, X was associated with Y
(r = 0.31; 95% CI 0.10–0.49) [7].”

The observational scope and uncertainty remain. Do not change this to “X causes Y.”

**Input:** “仅在数据集 A 上，准确率从 90% 降至 89%，延迟从 100 ms 降至 60 ms。
我们只是做了一个微不足道的尝试。”

**Revision:** “在数据集 A 上，延迟从 100 ms 降至 60 ms，准确率从 90% 降至 89%。”

The weaker accuracy remains visible; no general superiority or causal explanation
has been added. These example values belong only to these examples.
